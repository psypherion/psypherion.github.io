One straight line, y = mx + c — four representational spaces

The relation never changes. What changes is the space you choose to write it in. Move m, c, θ, φ below and watch the same invariant line turn into a rational function of θ, a clipped surface in spherical coordinates, and — one level further — a geodesic on a curved manifold that looks straight in one chart and sinusoidal in another.

Level 1

Same flat plane, three coordinate systems

Cartesian: y = mx + c, two numbers, done. Polar: r = c / (sinθ − m·cosθ) — the same points, but now r swings between ±∞ every half turn. Spherical: leave z free and the line becomes the plane traced by r(θ, φ) below, clipped to a sphere just to keep it on screen.

The plotted shape of a line in the plane never changes — plotting it in polar coordinates gives you the same straight line back. What changes is the functional form: r as a function of θ is a simple line in Cartesian coordinates, but a function with two vertical asymptotes per turn in polar coordinates, at θ = arctan(m) and arctan(m) + 180°. Geometric note: since z = r·cosφ is left free, the "surface" traced above is not a new shape at all — it's the flat plane y = mx + c, running parallel to the z-axis. The intricacy lives entirely in r(θ, φ), not in the object itself. Surface clipped to |r| ≤ 4 for display; it truly runs to infinity wherever sinφ·(sinθ − m·cosθ) → 0.
Level 2

One step further — a geodesic on a curved manifold

A sphere's own "straight line" is a great circle — a geodesic. Below is one genuine great circle, tilted by inclination i and rotated by node longitude Ω, with a point at parameter t. The same point is drawn three ways: on the sphere itself, on an ordinary latitude–longitude (equirectangular) chart, and on a gnomonic chart centered at the north pole. Gnomonic projection is defined so that every geodesic through its center maps to a straight line — it is the sphere's analogue of Cartesian coordinates for this manifold. Equirectangular coordinates are its "polar coordinates": convenient, but they bend the same invariant path into a sine wave.

Notes

The same pattern shows up outside geometry too

In every case below, nothing about the underlying object changes — only the basis, frame, or chart used to describe it. Complexity turns out to be a property of the representation, not the thing represented.

  • Fourier basis A sharp spike in time is an infinite sum of sinusoids in frequency. A pure sinusoid in time is a single point in frequency. Same signal, opposite "complexity," depending only on which basis you decompose it in.
  • Normal modes Coupled oscillators look like a tangle of interacting equations in ordinary coordinates. Rotate into the eigenbasis (the normal modes) and they decouple into independent, trivial equations. The coupling was in the coordinates, not the physics.
  • Rotating frames A free particle moving in a perfectly straight line, described from a rotating frame, needs centrifugal and Coriolis terms to explain the exact same motion. The law didn't get more complicated — the frame did.
  • Conformal maps The real line under z → z² becomes a parabola; under z → 1/z it becomes a circle. Holomorphic maps preserve local angles but can bend a straight line into almost anything.
  • Accelerated frames (SR) Inertial, straight-line motion in flat spacetime, viewed from a uniformly accelerating (Rindler) frame, is described by hyperbolic worldlines and comes with its own horizon — the spacetime version of the geodesic-on-a-manifold panel above.
  • Number bases 0.1 is a single digit in base 10 and an infinitely repeating fraction in base 2. The number never changed; only the base you chose to write it in did.
